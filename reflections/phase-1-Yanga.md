# Phase 1 Reflection — Yanga

## What did I do?

During Phase 1, I contributed to almost every artefact produced by the team, although the POPIA Exposure Assessment was the only document that I developed largely from scratch. The remaining artefacts, including the Business Profile, Project Charter, Threat Model v0, and repository documentation, were joint efforts between my project partner and me. My role often involved reviewing, refining, challenging assumptions, and strengthening sections of the documentation after initial drafts had been produced.

One of the most significant contributions I made was proposing that Hattinie and I work independently on several Phase 1 activities before comparing and consolidating our work. Although this approach consumed more time than a traditional division of labour, it provided valuable insight because we approached the same problems from different perspectives. The process often felt like an extended brainstorming exercise that unfolded over several weeks. By comparing independent solutions, we were able to identify weaknesses, challenge assumptions, and arrive at stronger final artefacts than either of us may have produced individually.

Working in this way also helped me appreciate how different people can interpret the same requirements differently. Reviewing those differences forced us to justify our reasoning rather than simply accepting the first solution that appeared reasonable.

## What did I find hard?

Several aspects of Phase 1 were difficult for me. The most challenging areas included distinguishing business requirements from technical solutions, understanding POPIA exposure within a business context, and developing a threat model.

Although I have studied these concepts before, applying them within a realistic scenario was significantly more difficult than understanding them in theory. In many cases, my instinct was to propose the most advanced or widely respected technical solutions I knew from books, training materials, and coursework. This is not the first time I have encountered this challenge. I experienced something similar during our Year 1 project and believed I had already learned that lesson. However, when this project began, I found myself falling back into the same pattern. The experience reinforced that architecture should begin with understanding business requirements and risks before discussing technologies or technical solutions.

I believe my lack of real-world experience contributed to this difficulty. I am grateful that this project is a simulated exercise because, if it were a real production planning project, I would not have felt confident in the quality of my initial decisions. The exercise exposed how much I still need to learn about translating theoretical knowledge into practical decision-making.

I also found the Git-based workflow challenging. While I am reasonably comfortable working in Linux environments and using command-line tools, I do not use Git's command-line workflow frequently enough for it to feel natural. As a result, I often needed to revisit commands, troubleshoot errors, and refresh my memory. The challenge was therefore less about Linux itself and more about developing confidence and consistency with Git and collaborative version-control practices.

The evidence requirements also caused confusion. Toolchain verification initially seemed more complicated than it actually was. Even after reading and revisiting the project documentation multiple times, I misunderstood what was being asked. My first interpretation was that toolchain verification would require something similar to a programming "Hello World" implementation to prove that the tools were working. I was relieved to discover that the requirement was primarily to demonstrate that the required tools were present and accessible rather than to build a complete proof-of-concept with each one.

Pipeline evidence created even more uncertainty. I spent considerable time trying to determine whether I was expected to create a separate artefact to prove that the pipeline was working. As someone approaching the requirement as a beginner, GitHub Actions already appeared to provide stronger and more trustworthy evidence than anything I could manually create. My confusion came from the feeling that perhaps there was an additional requirement I had overlooked.

Finally, I found the reflection requirement itself challenging. My natural tendency is to focus on completing a task and moving on to the next one. Taking time to reflect on what I did, how I approached it, and what I learned was unfamiliar to me. To overcome this, I had to transform the reflection headings into smaller questions that I could answer more naturally.

## What would I do differently?

If I were to restart Phase 1 tomorrow, the first thing I would do would be to read every project document and addendum carefully before starting any work. Rather than reading documents in a fragmented and reactive manner, I would systematically identify every requirement, build an action plan, and organise tasks into a logical sequence.

I would also highlight all points of uncertainty much earlier and actively seek clarification from lecturers. During this phase, I was excited about the project and often found myself jumping between tasks that seemed achievable rather than following a structured plan. In hindsight, this created unnecessary confusion and rework.

For Phase 2, I would review templates and repository guidance much earlier and more thoroughly before creating any deliverables. I would verify requirements before writing and would be more willing to arrange discussions with lecturers instead of allowing deadlines to create unnecessary pressure. I would also schedule more frequent meetings with Hattinie if we continue using the same independent-development approach because regular checkpoints would help us identify differences and merge work more efficiently.

## What do I understand now that I did not before?

One of the biggest lessons reinforced during this phase was that architecture should be driven by business requirements and risks rather than technology preferences. This was not entirely new to me because I encountered a similar lesson during our Year 1 project. However, Phase 1 demonstrated how easy it is to revert to thinking about technical solutions before fully understanding the business context. Working through the Business Profile, Threat Model, and Charter helped reinforce the importance of establishing the business problem before proposing solutions.

I also have a better understanding of threat modelling. Previously, I associated threat modelling primarily with vulnerabilities and security controls. I now understand that the process begins much earlier by identifying assets, actors, entry points, and business risks before discussing specific controls or technologies.

My perspective on evidence also evolved during this phase. Initially, I focused heavily on producing polished outputs and final results. Through the project requirements and discussions around evidence, I began to appreciate the value of preserving the process that led to those results. Git history, terminal recordings, logs, reports, and pipeline records provide context that final documents alone cannot. While I am still developing my understanding of evidence collection, I now recognise that documenting the journey, including mistakes, revisions, and troubleshooting, can be just as valuable as documenting successful outcomes.

This remains a work in progress for me. I still find it difficult to be comfortable with uncertainty, especially when working with unfamiliar concepts or tools. However, I am becoming more aware that learning often involves attempting solutions, making mistakes, asking questions, and refining my understanding rather than expecting complete confidence from the beginning.
